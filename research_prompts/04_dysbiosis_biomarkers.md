# Prompt 4: Biomarkers of dysbiosis in a patient with no history

Paste everything below the line into a deep-research tool. Use the strongest model you have.

---

## Role

You are a careful research analyst with a background in clinical microbiology, gastroenterology, oral medicine and laboratory medicine (analytical validity, reliability and clinical utility of tests). **The goal is to find out which measurements are genuinely informative and which are noise, not to promote any test.** Report what contradicts the idea of dysbiosis testing as readily as what supports it, and say plainly when the evidence is thin.

## The scenario

An adult attends a clinic. The clinician suspects dysbiosis. **The patient cannot answer any questions** about diet, lifestyle, medicines, supplements, environment, exposures, symptoms or timeline, and there is no way to measure their surroundings. The only thing the clinician can do is **test the patient**: stool, breath, saliva or oral swab, blood, urine, skin.

The question: **which biomarkers of dysbiosis, if any, are reliable and informative enough to use under these conditions, and how can results be interpreted when the usual history is missing?**

## What to find

**A. What "dysbiosis" means and whether it can be measured at all.** Consensus statements, definitions and critiques. Is there any validated, clinically accepted definition? What do systematic reviews say about "dysbiosis indices"? Say plainly if the term is considered too vague to measure.

**B. Gut biomarkers.** For each of: faecal calprotectin, faecal lactoferrin, hydrogen and methane breath tests (and emerging hydrogen sulfide breath testing), faecal microbiome sequencing summaries (diversity measures, "dysbiosis" or health indices), short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, urinary indican and organic acid tests, secretory IgA, and intestinal-permeability markers (zonulin, lipopolysaccharide-binding protein, soluble CD14, lactulose-mannitol test). Report: what it measures; **analytical validity** (repeatability, between-laboratory agreement); **clinical validity** (the condition it is validated for, thresholds, sensitivity and specificity, in which population); **clinical utility** (does acting on the result improve outcomes?); and whether guidelines recommend it.

**C. Oral biomarkers.** Volatile sulfur compound meters (Halimeter-type and gas-chromatography devices), organoleptic scoring, tongue-coating scores, salivary pH, qPCR panels for periodontal and sulfur-producing bacteria, point-of-care periodontal markers. Same questions as B. In particular: **how well do a person's own judgement and measured breath sulfide agree**, and how much do readings vary within a person across days, time of day and sampling method.

**D. Skin, systemic and routine blood markers.** Photographic scoring, validated symptom instruments, transepidermal water loss and sebum measures (where used to track change), non-specific inflammation markers (high-sensitivity CRP, ESR, ferritin) and routine blood tests that rule out common mimics (blood count, iron, B12, thyroid, liver, glucose). What does each add, and what common causes can it miss or mimic?

**E. Variability and noise: the most important section.** For every biomarker above: **test–retest reliability and within-person day-to-day variability** (coefficient of variation, intraclass correlation, minimal detectable change); effects of diet, fibre, antibiotics, bowel habit and stool form, time of day, fasting, brushing, mouthwash, season and illness; sample collection, storage and transport effects; and **between-laboratory differences, including studies that sent split samples to several commercial labs**. State how large a change must be before it exceeds noise.

**F. Interpreting results with no history.** This is the central problem. For each biomarker, how much is it distorted by factors the clinician cannot ask about (diet, recent antibiotics, acid-suppressing medicines, laxatives, probiotics, alcohol, smoking, bowel preparation, recent illness, sample timing)? Which markers are **robust** to unknown confounders and which are not? And which **objective tests can stand in for the missing history**: for example markers of alcohol use (GGT, mean cell volume, carbohydrate-deficient transferrin, phosphatidylethanol), tobacco exposure (cotinine), recent antibiotic or acid-suppressant effect, and dietary-intake markers. For each stand-in: accuracy, false positives and mimics, and what it can and cannot rule out.

**G. Commercial and direct-to-consumer tests.** Published evaluations of stool, oral and "gut health" tests sold to consumers: agreement between providers, reproducibility, accuracy against reference methods, and the positions of professional bodies, guideline groups and regulators (Australia, the United States, the United Kingdom, the European Union). Include documented harms (unnecessary supplements, anxiety, over-interpretation, unnecessary referrals).

**H. One result versus repeated testing.** What can be concluded from a single test, and what needs repeats? Published longitudinal single-person studies and n-of-1 trials: which markers were stable enough to detect change, how many repeats and what spacing were needed, and what the authors concluded about interpreting one person's data. What would a credible follow-up schedule look like?

**I. The best case against.** The strongest evidence that dysbiosis biomarkers are not useful for an individual patient: poor reproducibility, no accepted thresholds, no proven benefit from acting on results, and the risk of over-treatment. Present it fairly and in full. Include trials in which microbiome-guided or biomarker-guided management did or did not improve outcomes.

## Rules of evidence

1. Rank sources by strength of evidence: systematic reviews, guidelines and consensus statements first, then diagnostic-accuracy and reliability studies, then cohorts, then everything else. **Anything that carries a conclusion needs a PMID, a DOI or, for guidelines and regulator documents, a stable URL, and you must have opened it.** Say whether you read the full text or only the abstract. Lower-ranked sources may be listed as leads, marked as such, and must not support any conclusion.
2. **Copy the title, first author, year and journal from the record itself.** Never from memory.
3. Quote the sentence that reports each key number or finding. If you can't, mark the item UNVERIFIED and leave it out of your conclusions.
4. State the **study type** for each finding (randomised trial, cohort, cross-sectional, diagnostic-accuracy study, reliability study, guideline, consensus statement, review, case report) and the **population** (healthy adults, patients with a named condition, children).
5. Report only searches you actually ran and databases you could actually reach.
6. Vendor pages, laboratory marketing, wellness blogs, influencer posts and AI-written summaries are leads only, never evidence. **Do not use a laboratory's own interpretive text as evidence for its test.**
7. If you didn't find something, write "not found in these searches", never "doesn't exist".
8. **Do not stretch.** If a marker is validated for one condition (for example calprotectin to separate inflammatory bowel disease from irritable bowel syndrome), say so, and do not extend it to "dysbiosis" in general. If evidence is only associational, only in animals, or only in unrelated populations, say so in those words.
9. Give numbers (reliability coefficients, cut-offs, sensitivity and specificity) with their units, the population and the source sentence.

## Output

1. **Direct answer** (300 words): with no history available, is there any biomarker, or small set, that is reliable enough to use and informative enough to act on? State what is **established**, what is **plausible** and what is **untested**, using those three words. Include the single strongest argument against.
2. **Search log:** source, date, exact query, hits, kept.
3. **Gut markers (B):** table with biomarker | what it measures | validated for | analytical reliability | thresholds | sensitivity and specificity | clinical utility | study type | read in full or abstract.
4. **Oral markers (C)**, and **skin, systemic and routine blood markers (D):** same table form, plus agreement between self-judgement and measurement for oral markers.
5. **Variability and noise (E):** one table giving, for each biomarker, within-person variability, minimal detectable change, and main confounders.
6. **Robustness without history (F):** table of each marker against each unknown confounder (distorts it a lot / a little / not known), then a table of the stand-in tests for missing history with their accuracy and limits.
7. **Commercial tests (G):** what exists, with quotations.
8. **One result versus repeats (H):** what can and cannot be concluded, with published examples.
9. **The case against (I).**
10. **A tiered testing approach as the evidence supports it:** first-line (established, validated tests), second-line (plausible, with stated limits), and **not recommended** (with reasons). For each test: sample type, who can order it, approximate cost range, how often to repeat it, and the size of change that exceeds noise. Keep the first tier to no more than six tests.
11. **Gaps and next steps, ranked:** the five most useful things a clinician or small research group could do or ask for.
12. **Verification table:** identifier (PMID, DOI or URL) | title as on the record | first author | year | full text or abstract.

Describe test performance and what guidelines say. Do not suggest diagnoses or treatments for any individual.
