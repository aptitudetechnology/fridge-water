# Prompt 2: What evidence and data are we missing?

Paste everything below the line into a deep-research tool. Use the strongest model you have.

---

## Role

You are a careful research analyst with a background in drinking-water engineering, appliance regulation and infection control. This is for an independent, open-science project and a video essay about refrigerator water dispensers. **The goal is to find out whether the hypothesis holds up, not to confirm it.** Report what contradicts it as readily as what supports it.

## The project's hypothesis

- **Measured (one study):** water from in-refrigerator dispensers carried far more bacteria than the tap supplying it, and no chlorine, in ten homes (Swanson & He 2024, first-draw samples).
- **Hypothesis (untested):** over years of daily use, that water harms some people, and bad breath may be one early sign.

This prompt is not about proving the second half. It asks you to find **what is missing from our evidence base**: studies, standards, records and data sources we haven't found, and the strongest case against us.

## What we already have (find more; do not repeat these)

Identifiers were checked against PubMed or Crossref by the project.

- Swanson & He 2024, *Am J Infect Control*, PMID 38460731 (ten homes, Knoxville, Tennessee).
- Zhou et al. 2021, household carbon purifiers, PMID 33486296.
- Thomson et al. 2025, *M. abscessus* in drinking water including plumbed-in fridge taps, PMID 40072241.
- Gomez-Smith et al. 2015, biofilm communities in a chloraminated distribution system, PMID 26098899.
- Payment et al. 1991, household point-of-use filter units and illness, PMID 2059052.
- Lugli et al. 2022, tap-water bacteria and the gut, PMID 35355372. Parkin et al. 2026, infant dechlorination trial, PMID 42129350.
- Hile et al. 2025, review of about 70 water-dispenser studies, PMID 41522444. **Use its reference list, not its text:** some in-text attributions in it are wrong.
- Dispenser studies: Liguori 2010, Zanetti 2009, Sacchetti 2014, Girolamini 2019, Baumgartner 2006, Lévesque 1994.

## What to find

**A. More measurement studies.** Any study, in any country or language, that measured bacteria, fungi or chlorine in water or ice from in-fridge dispensers, in-line filters or ice makers. Include theses, conference abstracts, utility and regulator surveys, hospital infection-control reports and consumer-organisation tests (for example Choice, Which?, Consumer Reports). State the method for each, and mark grey-literature items as leads. Specifically look for the 2022 dissertation by C. S. Swanson (University of Tennessee).

**B. Standards and manuals.** What do fridge-filter certification standards (for example NSF/ANSI 42, 53, 58 and 372, and Australian and EU equivalents) actually test? Do any test for bacterial regrowth after dechlorination, over the filter's life? What do manufacturers' manuals say about flushing, cleaning and filter-change intervals, and do any mention bacterial growth? Quote the text and cite the document.

**C. Incident, complaint and recall records.** Search recall and complaint databases for fridge dispensers, filters and ice makers: the US CPSC (SaferProducts.gov), the Australian ACCC Product Safety site, the EU Safety Gate, Health Canada and any national equivalents. Also published outbreak or cluster reports tied to ice or water dispensers (for example *MMWR*, *Eurosurveillance*, *Journal of Hospital Infection*). For each database, give the exact URL, the search terms you used, the number of hits, and how a layperson can repeat the search.

**D. Practitioner knowledge.** Published or trade-press accounts by appliance technicians, plumbers or water-treatment professionals about slime, odour or contamination in fridge water lines; manufacturer service bulletins; trade-body guidance. Say who holds this knowledge and what a layperson could ask them. Do not invent quotes.

**E. How people actually use them.** Survey or market data on how often owners change fridge filters, whether they flush new filters, how much dispenser water people drink, and how many homes have in-fridge dispensers (by country). This tests whether "neglected" is a realistic case or a rare one.

**F. Home and community testing methods.** Evidence on how accurately low-cost methods count bacteria in water: plate counts (R2A against plate count agar, incubation temperature and time), ATP luminometry, and dip-slide kits. What is their reliability against laboratory methods, and what detection limits do they have?

**G. The best case against us.** The strongest published arguments and data that fridge-dispenser water is not a significant problem: studies finding no difference from tap, the WHO position that plate counts are not health-based indicators, and evidence that healthy people tolerate these exposures. Present them fairly and in full.

## Rules of evidence

1. Every study needs a PMID or DOI, and you must have opened its record. Say whether you read the full text or only the abstract.
2. **Copy the title, first author, year and journal from the record itself.** Never from memory and never from the list above.
3. Quote the sentence that reports each key number or requirement. If you can't, mark the item UNVERIFIED and leave it out of your conclusions.
4. Report only searches you actually ran, and databases you could actually reach.
5. Vendor pages, filter-seller blogs, influencer content and AI-written summaries are leads only, never evidence.
6. If you didn't find something, write "not found in these searches", never "doesn't exist".

## Output

1. **Direct answer** (200 words): the most important gaps found, and the single strongest argument against the hypothesis.
2. **Search log:** source, date, exact query, hits, kept.
3. **New studies:** table with study | appliance type | what was measured | method | key result | read in full or abstract.
4. **Standards and manuals:** what is tested and what is not, with quotations.
5. **Records and databases:** what exists, how to search it, and what the counts show.
6. **Practitioner and usage data:** what is documented, and what we would need to ask.
7. **Testing methods:** accuracy and limits of low-cost methods.
8. **The case against:** the strongest counter-evidence, fairly stated.
9. **Next steps, ranked:** the five most useful things a layperson could do or ask for, given what you found.
10. **Verification table:** identifier | title as on the record | first author | year | full text or abstract.
