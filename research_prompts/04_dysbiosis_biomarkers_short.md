# Prompt 4 (short): Biomarkers of dysbiosis in a patient with no history

Paste everything below the line into a deep-research tool. Use the strongest model you have.

---

## Role

You are a careful analyst in clinical microbiology and laboratory medicine. The goal is to find out which tests are genuinely informative and which are noise, not to promote any test. Report evidence against as readily as evidence for.

## Scenario

An adult attends a clinic and the clinician suspects dysbiosis. The patient can give **no history** (diet, medicines, lifestyle, environment, symptoms), and their surroundings can't be measured. The clinician can only test the patient: stool, breath, saliva, blood, urine, skin. **Which biomarkers, if any, are reliable and informative enough to use, and how can results be interpreted without history?**

## Find

1. Can dysbiosis be defined and measured at all? Consensus statements and critiques.
2. For each main biomarker (faecal calprotectin, hydrogen and methane breath tests, stool microbiome sequencing, short-chain fatty acids, permeability markers such as zonulin, oral sulfur-compound meters, hs-CRP and routine blood tests): what it is validated for, thresholds, sensitivity and specificity, and whether acting on it improves outcomes.
3. Reliability: test–retest and within-person variation, between-laboratory agreement (including split-sample studies of commercial labs), and the size of change that exceeds noise.
4. Confounders the clinician can't ask about (diet, antibiotics, acid-suppressing medicines, alcohol, smoking, probiotics): which markers are robust to them, and which objective tests (for example alcohol or nicotine markers) can stand in for the missing history.
5. Consumer microbiome tests: what guidelines and regulators say, and documented harms.
6. The strongest case against using any of this in an individual.

## Rules

1. Rank sources by strength of evidence: systematic reviews, guidelines and consensus statements first, then diagnostic-accuracy and reliability studies, then cohorts, then everything else. **Anything that carries a conclusion needs a PMID, a DOI or, for guidelines and regulator documents, a stable URL, and you must have opened it.** Say whether you read the full text or only the abstract. Lower-ranked sources are leads only.
2. Copy titles, authors, year and journal from the record, never from memory. Quote the sentence for each key number. If you can't, mark it UNVERIFIED and leave it out of your conclusions.
3. State the study type and population. Vendor pages, laboratory marketing, blogs and AI summaries are leads, never evidence.
4. **Do not stretch.** A marker validated for one condition is not validated for "dysbiosis". If you found nothing, write "not found in these searches".

## Output

1. **Direct answer** (200 words): what is **established**, **plausible** and **untested**, and the single strongest argument against.
2. **One table:** biomarker | sample | validated for | reliability | sensitivity and specificity | clinical utility | source (identifier; full text or abstract).
3. **Robustness to unknown confounders,** and the stand-in tests for missing history, with their limits.
4. **A tiered approach:** first-line (no more than five tests), second-line, and not recommended. For each: sample, cost range, how often to repeat.
5. **Verification table:** identifier | title | first author | year | full text or abstract.

Describe test performance and what guidelines say. Do not suggest diagnoses or treatments for any individual.
