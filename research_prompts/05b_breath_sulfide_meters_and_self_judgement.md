# Prompt 5b: Breath sulfide meters: how reliable are they, and how well can people judge their own breath?

Paste everything below the line into a deep-research tool. Use the strongest model you have. (Run second of three follow-ups.)

---

## Role

You are a careful analyst in oral medicine and laboratory medicine. The goal is to find out how reliable breath-sulfide measurement is and how well people can judge their own breath, not to promote any device. Report evidence against as readily as evidence for.

## Context

An adult wants objective measurements of their own bad breath that can be repeated over time. A previous review of dysbiosis biomarkers **could not find usable sources on oral volatile sulfur compound (VSC) meters.** "Not found in these searches" means a gap in the search, not evidence that meters are useless, so never turn it into a recommendation against them.

## Find

1. **Validity of VSC meters.** Halimeter-type monitors, OralChroma and other gas-chromatography devices, portable consumer meters, and **low-cost metal-oxide or electrochemical ("e-nose") hydrogen-sulfide sensors**: agreement with trained-judge organoleptic scoring, accuracy against gas chromatography, cut-offs, sensitivity and specificity, drift and humidity effects, and any validation in people.
2. **Reliability in one person.** Test–retest and day-to-day variation, and the effect of time of day, fasting, meals, brushing, mouthwash, tongue scraping, smoking, mouth breathing and sampling technique. **How large a change exceeds noise**, and how many repeats and what standardised protocol are needed.
3. **Self-judgement.** How well do people judge their own breath (cupped-hand test, wrist lick, spoon test, asking others) compared with a meter or a trained judge? Evidence on olfactory adaptation, and on people who believe they have bad breath when they don't, and the reverse.
4. **Where the sulfur comes from.** Evidence on tongue-coating bacteria and VSC; on blood-borne (extra-oral) sources; on hydrogen sulfide in breath from the gut (including hydrogen sulfide breath testing); and on **ratios of hydrogen sulfide to methyl mercaptan** as a clue to source.
5. **Common non-microbial causes** that a measured result can't distinguish (for example periodontal disease, tonsil stones, dry mouth, reflux, diabetes, medicines).
6. **The best case against** relying on meters or self-tracking.

## Rules

1. Rank sources by strength of evidence: systematic reviews, guidelines and consensus statements first, then diagnostic-accuracy and reliability studies, then cohorts, then everything else. **Anything that carries a conclusion needs a PMID, a DOI or, for guidelines and regulator documents, a stable URL, and you must have opened it.** Say whether you read the full text or only the abstract. Lower-ranked sources are leads only.
2. Copy titles, authors, year and journal from the record, never from memory. Quote the sentence for each key number. If you can't, mark it UNVERIFIED and leave it out of your conclusions.
3. State the study type and population. Manufacturer pages, wellness blogs and AI summaries are leads, never evidence.
4. **Do not stretch.** Say if evidence is only from small samples, one device or one clinic. If you found nothing, write "not found in these searches".

## Output

1. **Direct answer** (150 words): is breath-sulfide measurement reliable enough to track one person over time? **Established**, **plausible**, **untested**, and the strongest argument against.
2. **Device table:** device | what it measures | agreement with judges or GC | cut-offs | sensitivity and specificity | source (identifier; full text or abstract).
3. **Reliability table:** factor | effect on reading | size | source, and a **recommended standard protocol** if the evidence supports one.
4. **Self-judgement:** what the studies show, with numbers.
5. **Sources of sulfur** and **non-microbial causes:** short tables.
6. **The case against.**
7. **Verification table:** identifier | title | first author | year | full text or abstract.

Describe measurement performance and what the evidence shows. Do not suggest diagnoses or treatments for any individual.
