# Has anyone measured it? A baseline PubMed search (4 Nov 2026)

Question: has anyone measured whether drinking water that carries microbes (including water from fridge dispensers and carbon filters) alters the gut microbiome, gut barrier, inflammation, or markers of biological ageing?

## The answer

**Partly.** Several pieces exist. As far as this search shows, **no study has measured the whole chain** (dispenser or filter-water microbes in the home, then the gut microbiome or inflammation, then ageing markers, in the same people over time). So "nobody has measured it" was too strong, and "nobody has measured the whole chain" is what I can support.

## How this was done, and its limits

- 10 structured searches of PubMed (title and abstract fields), run on 4 Nov 2026. I read the abstracts of the most relevant hits through PubMed.
- **This is not a systematic review.** One database, 10 queries, the first 12 results of each (sorted by relevance), no full texts, no Embase or Scopus, no citation chasing, no trial registries, no non-English search. Treat it as a baseline to compare the deep-research result against.
- Statements below are what the abstracts say. "Title only" means I haven't read the abstract.
- The ageing and inflammation searches (queries 9 and 10) found nothing relevant to water microbes: the hits were about arsenic and telomeres, or about other topics.

## What this means

| Link in the story | Status after this search |
|---|---|
| Fridge dispensers raise potentially pathogenic bacteria at the tap | **Supported** by one peer-reviewed short report (Swanson & He 2024) and a household-filter metagenomic study (Zhou 2021) |
| Water bacteria can settle in the human gut | **Possible, shown in one person** (Lugli 2022) |
| Water source and treatment are associated with gut microbiome differences | **Documented in several observational studies and trials**; effects of chlorination looked small in children and infants |
| Removing chlorine alone changes the infant gut microbiome | **One 12-month blinded trial found no overall difference** (Parkin 2026); antibiotic-resistance pathways differed |
| Dispenser-water microbes at realistic doses, over years, change gut microbiome, inflammation or ageing markers in healthy adults | **Not found in these searches** |

## What it changes in the story

- "Nobody has measured it" should read "no study I found has measured the whole chain." The animation and script are updated to say so.
- The 2024 in-fridge dispenser study is the strongest piece of fridge-specific evidence so far. It needs reading in full.
- The finding that chlorination, and its removal, appears to have small effects on the gut microbiome in children and infants is a caution for the hypothesis. It doesn't address regrowth in a carbon filter, but it means the "dysbiosis" arrow is not obviously strong.
- The Lugli case is a good reason to keep the colonisation question open, and a reason to soften "most of it is gone within days."

## Full-text read: Parkin et al. 2026 (Commun Med 6:405, PMID 42129350), 4 Nov 2026

Read from `sources/s43856-026-01626-2.pdf`. Free to read; data on OSF (osf.io/krjbe) and PRJNA1452547.

**What it is.** Double-blind RCT in Perth (TUMS), nested in the ORIGINS cohort. 197 infants randomised at 6 months: 99 to a benchtop carbon-block filter that removed chlorine (rated >99% chlorine), 98 to an identical sediment-only sham filter. Run for 12 months. Free chlorine in tap water 0.3 to 1.1 mg/L; the filter cut total chlorine from 0.74 to 0.10 mg/L. Stool samples: 170 at baseline, 130 at the end (65 per arm; 66% retention, partly due to COVID).

**What it found.**
- No difference in community structure: beta diversity 0.56% of variance, p = 0.84; richness p = 0.43; Shannon p = 0.12.
- 55 species differed (adjusted p < 0.01), mostly rare and individual to each infant. The authors say the shifts "vary substantially from infant to infant".
- The "Antibiotic resistance" MetaCyc group was higher with chlorinated water: log2 fold change 1.59, p = 0.0006, **adjusted p = 0.030**. One pathway (polymyxin resistance) adjusted p = 0.048. These are functional pathways, **not measured resistance genes**.
- Allergy, wheeze and eczema: no difference, but about 50% missing data.

**What to be careful about.**
1. **The abstract's "stronger with antibiotic use" does not survive correction.** Table 3: antibiotic-exposed log2FC 1.85, raw p = 0.016, **adjusted p = 0.16**; unexposed raw p = 0.248, adjusted p = 0.63. Neither is significant after adjustment. The authors call the results "hypothesis-generating".
2. **The main finding is borderline.** Adjusted p of 0.03 to 0.05, 65 per arm, 14 species already differed at baseline, and antibiotic use could not be a covariate.
3. **The filter removed more than chlorine** (nitrates, metals, by-products). The authors say so.
4. **Direction for our story:** the chlorinated (control) arm had *more* resistance-linked pathways. The dechlorinated arm had *fewer*. That is the opposite of "removing chlorine harms the gut", for infants and at this chlorine level.
5. **No microbial counts were reported for the filtered or tap water.** A text search found no mention of heterotrophic plate counts, CFU, biofilm or regrowth. The study tells us nothing about whether the filtered water carried more bacteria than tap water. It is a chlorine-removal trial, not a regrowth trial. It used a fresh 12-month filter in a clean-supply setting.
6. Infants of 6 to 18 months, on milk and solids, with very different gut ecology from adults.

**Verdict for the script.** Accurate as currently written ("no overall difference after 12 months"). Do not use "stronger with antibiotic use" without saying it was not significant after correction. It also does not support the dispenser hypothesis in either direction; its value is that a clean, blinded trial of dechlorination found a small functional signal and no structural one.

The authors also state the trial was "the first randomised controlled trial" of its kind and that Nadimpalli 2022 found similar functional shifts in Bangladesh.

## Full-text read: Lugli et al. 2022 (Environ Microbiol 24:3912-23, PMID 35355372), 4 Nov 2026

Read from `sources/lugli2022.pdf` (open access, CC BY). Sequence data: PRJNA806724.

**What was done.**
1. 16 water samples from Parma, Italy (12 public fountains, 4 household taps), shallow shotgun metagenomics plus flow cytometry.
2. 115 more drinking-water datasets from nine public projects in seven countries, to define a "core" tap-water microbiota. The top genera were Bradyrhizobium, Sphingomonas, Pseudomonas, Novosphingobium and Paraburkholderia; each appeared in about 90 to 99% of samples.
3. In silico: reads from 196 public human stool metagenomes were mapped to genome fragments assembled from the water. Matches (at least 99% identity, at least 10 reads) were found in **46 of 196** stool samples.
4. **The pilot:** one healthy adult man who had drunk the same tap water (W001) for three years. Stool at T1 (drinking), T2 (after 2 weeks on bottled water, which had negligible microbial DNA) and T3 (back on tap). qPCR for three water bacteria.

**What it found.**
- Water cell counts, from flow cytometry (reported as "predicted CFU/mL"): 2×10³ to 5.3×10⁶ per mL; **2.8×10⁴ per mL excluding the outlier W001**. W001, the pilot subject's own tap, was the outlier at 5.3×10⁶ (4.8×10⁶ at T3).
- Of the three bacteria tested by qPCR in stool, **Bradyrhizobium and Sphingobium were below the detection limit. Only Curvibacter was found**, at all three time points, including after the washout.
- Authors' own words: "apparent colonization"; "even if these data have been obtained from a single individual, they suggest..."; and "a clinical trial ... needs to be performed in order to corroborate our data."

**What to be careful about.**
1. **The abstract's "proof for ... colonization" is stronger than the body.** The body says "possible", "apparent", "suggest". One person, one species, one two-week washout, no pre-exposure baseline.
2. Persistence for two weeks after the washout is consistent with colonisation, but also with slow clearance, or with intake from other sources (food, watering plants, which the authors raise for the stool matches).
3. The 46/196 matches are DNA read mapping, not live bacteria, and they could reflect the same environmental taxa arriving through food, soil or water. The paper cannot tell which.
4. Cells counted by flow cytometry include dead and unculturable cells, so "CFU" here means "cells". It is not comparable with a plate count.
5. Nothing was measured on gut health, inflammation, or any outcome. Authors speculate that colonisers "may impact human health".
6. The water was municipal tap, not a dispenser. Bradyrhizobium, Sphingomonas and Pseudomonas are in nearly all tap water worldwide.

**Two things this changes in the story.**
- **Dose context.** Ordinary Parma tap water averaged about 2.8×10⁴ cells/mL, which is **inside our working range of 10³ to 10⁵** for a dispenser. A dispenser at those counts would not be unusual in its numbers. What may differ is the mix (Swanson & He found Mycobacterium and Pseudomonas up) and the loss of a chlorine residual. This also weakens any claim that such counts are exotic, and it fits the hormesis question: everyone drinks this.
- **Wording fixed.** Script, captions and audio said "bacteria from tap water living in one person's gut after three years". It now says one tap-water bacterium persisted in one person's stool after two weeks on bottled water. Updated in `script_v2.md`, `narration.md`, `fridge-water.html` (the captions, the tiers on-screen text and the aria-label), and the audio was regenerated for `inside-3` and `tiers-2`.

## Full-text read: Thomson et al. 2025 (Am J Respir Crit Care Med 211:842-853, PMID 40072241), 4 Nov 2026

*Infection by Clonally Related Mycobacterium abscessus Isolates: The Role of Drinking Water.* DOI 10.1164/rccm.202409-1824OC. Open access (CC BY), read from `sources/Thomson10346709.pdf`. This was not on my earlier list. It is the first paper in the project with a **fridge tap linked to a patient's own isolates**.

**What was done.** Queensland, Australia. 289 *M. abscessus* (MABS) isolates: 231 from respiratory samples (71 people with cystic fibrosis, 25 adults without CF) and 58 from drinking water (homes, hospitals, public taps and the Brisbane distribution system). Whole-genome sequencing compared them. Home sampling included 34 homes, with extra outlets such as filtered drinking water (n = 8), **plumbed-in refrigerator taps (n = 6)** and **one refrigerator water dispenser**.

**What it found.**
- MABS was cultured from 12% of homes (4 of 34) and 4% of home tap and other point-of-use outlets (6 of 136). Half of all isolates fell into globally spread "dominant circulating clones". A water-linked clade (DCC5) carried extra copper-resistance genes.
- **The fridge case:** a person with CF and active MABS. "Seven of eight MABS isolates recovered from the patient's plumbed-in refrigerator tap (March 2017) were very closely related (5-15-SNP difference) to their earlier respiratory isolates (2012)." A second fridge-tap isolate was "a distinct, unrelated" strain, not found in the patient's sputum.
- The authors' conclusion: "drinking water is a reservoir for MABS and may be a vector in the chain of MABS infection."

**What to be careful about.**
1. **Direction is unproven, and the authors say so.** The pasted discussion says: "Water may have been the source of infection; however, patient cough aerosols may have inadvertently caused contamination." The sputum isolates were from 2012 and the fridge isolates from 2017, five years later, so the tap could have been contaminated by the patient. They also discuss "contamination crawl" and say it is unknown whether bacteria move upstream in pipes.
2. **The unrelated second strain is the better evidence.** It shows the fridge carried MABS that did not come from the patient. The authors use it to support water as an intermediary. It is one fridge.
3. **The fridge part is a tiny share of the data:** one patient. The study's main findings are about the water supply in general (two unconnected networks, and an apartment block that cultured 21 of 34 home isolates).
4. **Outcome is lung infection** in people with cystic fibrosis or bronchiectasis, not gut health, inflammation or ageing. The paper does not establish the route (swallowing, aspiration, or breathing aerosols). Fridge water is drunk, while the authors' wider examples include showers and a CPAP humidifier.
5. SNP-distance cutoffs can mislead, and the authors say so. Culture underestimates the true load.
6. The authors say plumbed-in refrigerators are "high-risk infection sources because of their complicated, narrow pipes with greater surface area for biofilm formation, where NTM may proliferate". That is their reasoning and it cites a general biofilm paper (ref 40), not a measurement of fridges.
7. *M. abscessus* is naturally resistant to chlorine and chloramine, so a filter removing disinfectant isn't the main issue for this organism. The point is that it lives in plumbing and grows in biofilm.

**What it adds to the story.**
- It backs the "established" claim that fridge plumbing can hold opportunistic mycobacteria, from a second, independent team, and it adds a **human case** for people with weak lungs. This matches Swanson & He 2024 (Mycobacterium up in dispensers).
- It supports the line "opportunists matter for weak immunity". It does **not** support any link to gut health, inflammation or ageing. Keep the two claims apart on screen.
- It also gives the project a fair question for the people who service or sell fridges: how often is the fridge tap the one that grows these organisms?

**A line the script could use (not yet added):** "In Queensland, genome sequencing found Mycobacterium abscessus in a cystic fibrosis patient's fridge tap, closely related to their own lung isolates. The authors say the patient may have contaminated the tap."

## Review: Hile et al. 2025 (AIMS Microbiol 11:891-914, PMID 41522444), 4 Nov 2026

*Microbiological quality of drinking water from water dispensers.* DOI 10.3934/microbiol.2025039, PMCID PMC12782947. Hile TD, Leal R, Dunbar SG, Sinclair RG. Open access; full text saved as `sources/Hile2025_PMC12782947.xml` (from Europe PMC). **Not in the project before 4 Nov 2026.** Read: abstract, methods, and the passages on dispensers against tap water. Tables and the reference list were not read in detail.

**What it is.** A literature review of about 70 studies on microbial contamination in "water dispensers", defined as water coolers: point-of-use units connected to the tap, and bottled-water units. Databases: PubMed, EBSCO, Google Scholar. It describes itself as using a "systematic search strategy" but is not a formal systematic review with a flow count. Its conclusion: dispensers "can harbor higher levels of microbial contamination than the tap water sources supplying them."

**Not about fridges.** A text search finds **no mention of "refrigerator" or "fridge"**, and none of Swanson & He. Its subject is office, school and public water coolers. It can support "dispensers can degrade water". It cannot be cited for in-fridge units.

**Studies it cites, as leads only (unchecked, taken from its own text):** Raymond et al. (carbon filters in point-of-use devices gave "1-2-fold increases" in bacterial concentration over tap water), Baumgartner et al. (Switzerland, dispensers mainly caused contamination), Precha et al. 2022 (Thailand, *Int J Prev Med*, dispensers more contaminated than the supplying water), Farhadkhani et al. (Iran), Hampikyan et al. 2017 (Turkey, ice machines, *J Water Health*), and a Brazilian study with 36.4% of tap samples and 76.6% of bottled-dispenser samples contaminated. These need checking against their own records before use.

**Caveats.**
- A review of reports that varied in design, with no combined analysis or flow count. The authors acknowledge the "paucity of publications" and that maintenance varied.
- Most findings are indicator organisms (coliforms, HPC, *Pseudomonas*) against regulatory limits, not gut or health outcomes.
- It says routine cleaning with disinfectants "significantly decreases bacterial contamination", and that delayed filter replacement can make filters "breeding grounds". That supports the "not blaming filter makers" framing: maintenance is the lever.

**Use.** It is a reasonable second-source citation for "dispensers can carry more bacteria than the tap supplying them", and a source of primary studies to read. It also gives a place to check the script's unsourced line about hospital ice and water dispensers (`script_v2.md` line 494): Hampikyan 2017 is about ice machines in food establishments, not hospitals.

### Update after reading the Hile 2025 PDF (`sources/10.3934_microbiol.2025039.pdf`), 4 Nov 2026

I read sections 3.2 to 3.4 and Table 3 and checked the reference list. This **corrects the lead list above.**

**Do not use:**
- **"Raymond et al. [18]: carbon filters in point-of-use devices gave 1-2-fold increases in bacterial concentration."** Reference 18 in the review's own list is Koufman & Johnston 2012, a paper on alkaline (pH 8.8) water and reflux. ## Full-text read: Swanson & He 2024 (Am J Infect Control 52:857-9, PMID 38460731), 5 Nov 2026

Read from `sources/2024_AJIC_Linkage of in-refrigerator water dispensing systems….pdf` (3 pages, including both figures). The first author is **Clifford S. Swanson, PhD**; the corresponding author is **Qiang He, PhD** (qianghe@utk.edu), University of Tennessee, Knoxville. Funding: US National Science Foundation award 2025339 and the University of Tennessee. Conflicts of interest: none declared. Sequence data: NCBI SRA, SRR22894290 to SRR22894309.

**What was done.**
- **10 single-family homes in Knoxville, Tennessee**, on public water with free chlorine, each with an in-refrigerator dispensing system (IRWDS) with a **carbon-block filter**.
- One 1-litre sample from each fridge dispenser and one from the cold tap of the same house, **early in the morning before any water was used** (so, first-draw, stagnant water).
- Measured: free chlorine (DPD colorimetric), heterotrophic plate count (HPC, CFU), and 16S rRNA gene sequencing (V4 region). Comparisons by Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

**What it found.**

| Measure | Tap | Fridge | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| HPC | 20 ± 38 CFU/100 mL (about **0.2 CFU/mL**) | 93,060 ± 167,387 CFU/100 mL (about **930 CFU/mL**, SD about 1,670) | p = 0.00195 (Fig 1A) |
| Free chlorine | 0.84 ± 0.18 ppm | **not detectable** | p < 0.001 (Fig 1B) |
| *Mycobacterium*, share of the community | 9.19 ± 11.82% | 6.68 ± 5.45% | p = 0.94, **no significant difference** (Fig 2A) |
| *Pseudomonas*, share of the community | 10.97 ± 12.11% | 0.04 ± 0.07% | p = 0.0073, **lower in the fridge** (Fig 2A) |
| Estimated fold change in abundance | | *Mycobacterium* 8,053; *Pseudomonas* 221 | (Fig 2B) |

- The authors' conclusion: IRWDS "resulted in elevated microbial contamination of drinking water", "likely due to the unintended removal of disinfectant residual by carbon-block filters".

**What I take from it, and what to be careful about.**
1. **The overall result is clear and consistent with our hypothesis.** The fridge water had thousands of times more culturable bacteria than the tap feeding it (ratio of means about 4,650), and no chlorine. The p-value of 0.00195 is exactly the smallest possible for a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on ten pairs. That suggests **every one of the ten fridges was higher than its tap**. This is my inference from the statistics, not a statement in the paper, so I haven't put it in the video.
2. **The headline "8,053-fold Mycobacterium, 221-fold Pseudomonas" is a constructed estimate.** It multiplies each genus's share from 16S sequencing by the total culturable count (the paper's Eq. 1). The shares themselves did not rise: *Mycobacterium* was unchanged and *Pseudomonas* fell. So the fold changes come from the whole community growing, not from these two genera taking over. Mixing a sequencing fraction with a plate count is also an approximation, because HPC only counts bacteria that grow on the medium used. Anyone who reads the paper will see this. Say "the whole community grew, and by the authors' estimate so did these two genera in absolute terms."
3. **Small and narrow.** Ten homes, one city, one sampling time, first-draw (not flushed) samples, one type of filter (carbon block). Filter age, brand and household use were not reported. HPC medium and incubation are only cited ("as previously described", ref 5). No medians or ranges are given, and the very large SD (larger than the mean) means a few homes were far higher than the rest.
4. **The mechanism is the authors' inference.** Chlorine loss and the HPC rise occurred together. Nothing was manipulated (for example, comparing a chlorine-removing and a sham filter).
5. **No health outcome, and nothing on sulphide, hydrogen sulfide, endotoxin, yeasts or moulds.** It says nothing about bad breath.
6. **Genus-level only.** 16S can't say whether the *Mycobacterium* are pathogenic species.

**Our dose assumption needs adjusting.** We have been using "10³ to 10⁵ CFU/mL" for a neglected dispenser. That came from Payment's household units. **The only fridge measurement is a mean of about 930 CFU/mL (first-draw, n = 10), with a large spread.** That is just under our lower bound on average, above the US 500 CFU/mL benchmark, and very probably higher in the worst homes. State it as "about a thousand CFU/mL on average, with some homes higher". The upper part of the old range is not shown for fridges.

**Where the project text was changed because of this:** the scene 3 and evidence-chart narration and captions, the landing page, the script, `test_plan.md`, and `research_prompts/01_whats_in_the_water.md`.

## PubMed search, 5 Nov 2026: bacteria in water and other health outcomes

**Method.** E-utilities searches of PubMed, run on 5 Nov 2026. A first set was too loose, because the search terms were only partly restricted to titles and abstracts. It returned mostly animal colitis and arsenic papers where "drinking water" was only the way a drug was given. I re-ran the outcome searches with every term restricted to title and abstract: (drinking water OR tap water OR water dispenser OR water cooler OR filtered water) AND (bacteria OR microbial OR biofilm OR microbiome OR heterotrophic) AND an outcome group. I read the 7 most relevant hits of each, by PubMed's relevance sort, and the abstracts of the promising ones. **This is a quick check, not a systematic review**: one database, title and abstract only, no citation chasing. The acronym "IRWDS" returns nothing because only Swanson & He use it.

**The outcome searches.** Hits for the water-and-bacteria terms combined with each outcome, and what was relevant among the top 7:

| Outcome | Hits | Relevant studies found |
|---|---|---|
| Skin (eczema, atopic dermatitis, acne, rosacea) | 14 | **One**: Turkalj 2020, below. It found *less* allergic disease with more bacteria |
| Liver (fatty liver, steatosis, liver enzymes) | 66 | None. Animal studies where water carries a treatment |
| Gut disease (IBS, IBD, calprotectin, permeability) | 256 | None. Animal colitis models |
| Inflammation markers (CRP, IL-6) | 68 | None |
| Brain, mood, cognition | 143 | None |
| Oral microbiome, halitosis, bad breath | 17 | None about water bacteria. Vanhaecke 2022 (gut, already known) came up |
| Chronic gastrointestinal symptoms | 429 | Mostly cholera and diarrhoeal-disease reviews, nothing on dispensers or filters |

**Not found in these searches:** any study linking bacteria in drinking water, or fridge or filter water, to liver disease, gut inflammation, systemic inflammation markers, mood or cognition, or bad breath. That does not mean none exists. Only the top 7 per search were read, and Embase, Scopus and Web of Science were not searched.

**What this changes for the project**
1. **The honest balance shifts slightly toward "it depends".** The one direct human study of bacteria in drinking water and a health outcome (Turkalj) found less allergy with more bacteria. That fits the "old friends" idea, and it's the strongest reason to take the hormesis question seriously, and to keep saying the harm side is a hypothesis.
2. **The evidence of harm remains mostly in vulnerable hospital patients** (Baker, Thomson, Lalande, Kanwar). That matches the video's label: the clearest risk is for weakened immunity.
3. **Nothing was found for the breath, liver, brain or inflammation links.** They remain untested, and the video's labels are accurate.
4. **A candidate for the video:** a short scene on the "old friends" counter-evidence, with Turkalj as the example. It would show the project deals fairly with data that cuts the other way.
