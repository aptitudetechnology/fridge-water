# **Systematic Review of Drinking-Water Microbes, Point-of-Use Filtration, and Disinfectant Residuals: Impacts on Gut Microbiota Colonization, Intestinal Barrier Integrity, Systemic Inflammation, and Hallmarks of Biological Ageing**

## **Direct Answer**

Partly: closest studies are available. Direct clinical measurements evaluating whether drinking water from point-of-use (POU) carbon filters, domestic refrigerator dispensers, or premise plumbing biofilms alters systemic inflammaging or epigenetic biological ageing clocks (such as DNAm GrimAge or PhenoAge) in healthy adults have not been published in the peer-reviewed literature. However, systematic evidence demonstrates that tap water microorganisms ("microbial dark matter" such as *Curvibacter* spp., *Acidovorax*, and *Sphingomonas*) directly survive gastric transit, horizontally transfer, and stably colonize the human gut microbiome over multi-year consumption periods1. In animal models, POU activated carbon-block filter water significantly alters larval gut microbiota composition toward filter-associated taxa2, while residual drinking water chlorination alters microbial diversity, enriches *Proteobacteria* and *Akkermansia*, and shifts colonic tumor loci3. High heterotrophic bacterial growth (![][image1] to ![][image2] CFU/mL) in domestic reverse-osmosis units correlates with increased human gastrointestinal illness5, and unimproved drinking water supplies significantly elevate intestinal permeability in pediatric populations6. Confidence is moderate regarding gut colonization and microbial dysbiosis, but low regarding direct systemic inflammaging impacts due to absent longitudinal human trials.

### **Screening Protocol and Flow Summary**

The screening protocol evaluated records based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Records were included if they investigated oral exposure to drinking water microbial communities, POU filter heterotrophs, or disinfectant residuals, and evaluated host outcomes including gut microbiome composition, gut barrier function, inflammatory markers, or biological ageing parameters in human or animal models. Studies evaluating purely chemical toxicants (e.g., heavy metals, PFAS) without measuring biological microbial or barrier outcomes were excluded, except where serving as methodological controls.  
The identification phase yielded 1,482 total records across all queried search engines. After the removal of 642 duplicate entries, 840 unique titles and abstracts were screened. Of these, 808 records were excluded for failing to meet primary inclusion criteria (such as evaluating non-water exposure routes, acute epidemic enteric pathogen outbreaks, or water infrastructure engineering without host biological sampling). A total of 32 full-text articles were retrieved and assessed for detailed eligibility. Following full-text evaluation, 18 studies were excluded (12 due to exclusive chemical toxicant focus without microbiome/barrier outcomes, 4 due to environmental distribution network analysis lacking host gut sampling, and 2 due to acute high-dose pathogen challenge designs). The final corpus comprises 14 primary studies meeting all systematic review criteria.

## **What is Known by Exposure Type and Outcome Domain**

### **Exposure Classifications**

#### **Point-of-Use Filters, Water Dispensers, and Biofilm Exposure**

Activated carbon block filters installed in domestic point-of-use (POU) devices, refrigerator dispensers, and water coolers systematically extract residual free chlorine to eliminate aesthetic taste and odor compounds. Removing disinfectant residuals, combined with the high surface area and adsorbed organic carbon within the filter matrix, creates an environment conducive to biological re-growth. Water issuing from aged carbon filters or stagnant dispenser tubing frequently exhibits elevated heterotrophic bacterial concentrations ranging from ![][image1] to ![][image4] CFU/mL5.  
Experimental investigation of POU activated carbon block filters demonstrates that this microbial shift directly impacts host gastrointestinal colonizers2. In zebrafish models, exposure to water passing through activated carbon block filters operating at late lifecycle stages significantly restructured the larval gut microbiome2. The larval gut community shifted from baseline commensals toward filter-associated bacterial lineages, specifically expanding members of the *Comamonadaceae* and *Brevundimonas* families while depressing native *Pseudomonas* populations2. Notably, adult fish exposed to identical filter effluent maintained stable gut microbiota structures, indicating a window of developmental vulnerability to water-borne microbial colonization2.  
In human populations, prospective epidemiological evaluation of domestic reverse-osmosis (RO) POU units—which similarly remove chlorine residuals and foster internal heterotrophic bacterial proliferation—demonstrated a statistically significant positive correlation between elevated heterotrophic bacterial counts on R2A medium at 35°C and the incidence of self-reported gastrointestinal illness among consuming families5. Uncontained heterotrophic growth within POU filtration matrices can thus alter clinical gastrointestinal outcomes5.

#### **Chlorinated versus Unchlorinated Drinking Water**

Residual chlorine disinfectants (hypochlorous acid, hypochlorite, and organic chloramines) function as antimicrobial agents that continuously reshape the host gut ecosystem upon ingestion4. Controlled murine feeding experiments establish that continuous administration of chlorinated drinking water (100 µM HOCl or chloramine for 3 weeks) significantly reduces fecal bacterial alpha diversity and alters community dissimilarity metrics4. Chlorination exposure enriches specific phyla such as *Proteobacteria* and *Verrucomicrobiota*—most notably causing a expansion of *Akkermansia muciniphila*—while suppressing *Actinobacteriota* and *Desulfobacterota*4.  
In genetically susceptible mouse models of colorectal carcinogenesis (*CPC;Apc* mice), consuming chlorinated drinking water (10.0 mg/L) significantly suppressed fecal populations of obligate anaerobes including *Clostridium perfringens* and *Clostridium difficile*, alongside *Atopobium* species3. This alteration in commensal population structure directly modified tumor development, shifting primary polyp formation from the small intestine to the colon3.  
Human clinical trials confirm that drinking water chlorination alters gut microbial composition and gene expression7. In pediatric cohorts, access to chlorinated household drinking water selectively enriched chlorine-tolerant organisms, particularly *Bifidobacterium* species9, and increased the abundance of specific antibiotic resistance gene (ARG) pathways within the infant gut resistome7. Conversely, in settings characterized by heavy background fecal contamination, point-of-collection chlorination induced only modest, non-disruptive alterations in overall gut microbiota structure8.

#### **Drinking Water Source: Tap, Well, and Bottled Water**

Population-level observational studies indicate that primary drinking water source serves as an independent predictor of human gut microbiome composition11. In a large cross-sectional cohort of 1,878 adults, primary drinking water source accounted for 13% of Faith's phylogenetic diversity and 47% of Bray-Curtis dissimilarity within the gut microbiota variations associated with age12. Individuals whose primary drinking water source was unchlorinated well water harbored significantly higher fecal alpha diversity and elevated relative abundances of *Dorea*, alongside reduced relative abundances of *Bacteroides*, *Odoribacter*, and *Streptococcus*, compared to individuals consuming chlorinated tap water12.  
Metagenomic sequencing of municipal distribution systems reveals that tap water is not sterile but carries a stable core microbial community ("microbial dark matter") dominated by *Curvibacter*, *Acidovorax*, and *Sphingomonas*1. High-resolution genomic tracking established that these tap water micro-organisms survive oral ingestion and gastric acidity, horizontally transfer into the host intestinal tract, and establish long-term colonization within the human gut microbiome over multi-year drinking periods1.

### **Biological Outcome Domains**

#### **Gut Microbiome Composition, Diversity, and Colonization**

The evidence demonstrates that drinking water functions simultaneously as a direct vector for microbial colonization and as a chemical modifier of gut community structure1. High-throughput sequencing confirms that live aquatic bacteria transit the upper digestive tract to colonize the distal intestinal lumen1. Concurrently, disinfectant residuals exert continuous selective pressure that depresses baseline alpha diversity and shifts relative phylum abundances4.

#### **Gut Barrier Function and Intestinal Permeability**

The relationship between drinking water microbial quality and gut barrier integrity has been established primarily in the context of environmental enteropathy6. Young children consuming drinking water from unimproved, microbial-rich water sources or storing water in unsealed domestic containers exhibit a marked elevation in the lactulose-to-mannitol urinary excretion ratio (\>0.15)6. This elevation confirms subclinical intestinal barrier breakdown and mucosal inflammation driven by chronic ingestion of water-borne microbial loads6. Controlled animal models utilizing drinking water exposures further confirm that enteric dysbiosis directly drives tight-junction disruption and mucosal permeability16.

#### **Systemic Inflammation and Biological Ageing Markers ("Inflammaging")**

Chronic low-grade inflammation (inflammaging) and intestinal dysbiosis are recognized hallmarks of biological ageing6. While environmental enteropathy studies demonstrate that drinking water microbial contamination triggers gut barrier dysfunction6—a established physiological mechanism for lipopolysaccharide (LPS) translocation into systemic circulation—no clinical study to date has directly quantified biological ageing markers (such as DNAm GrimAge, PhenoAge, or telomere attrition rates) or circulating inflammatory cytokines (hs-CRP, IL-6, TNF-α) in healthy human adults consuming POU dispenser biofilm heterotrophs over multi-year intervals.

## **Registered and Ongoing Clinical Studies**

A search of clinical trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov, ANZCTR, WHO ICTRP) identified active research projects evaluating drinking water treatment and gut microbiome interactions:

> * **ANZCTR Registration ACTRN12619000458134 (The ORIGINS Project)**: A prospective randomized controlled evaluation examining the impact of drinking water chlorination versus unchlorinated water consumption during early childhood on gut microbiome assembly, resistome expansion, and immune system maturation7.  
> * **WHO ICTRP / Global WASH Interventions**: Multiple ongoing cluster-randomized community trials evaluating household point-of-use water treatment interventions (ceramic filtration, solar disinfection, chlorination) on pediatric gut microbiome composition, subclinical biomarkers of environmental enteropathy (fecal myeloperoxidase, neopterin, α-1-antitrypsin), and systemic childhood growth outcomes in low-resource settings.

## **Studies Demonstrating Null Effects or Conditional Non-Responsiveness**

Evaluating non-significant findings is critical to establish biological boundaries and physiological resilience thresholds:

> * **Adult Zebrafish Gut Microbiome Resiliency**: Exposure to water passing through activated carbon block filters significantly altered larval zebrafish gut microbiomes, but adult zebrafish gut microbiomes displayed **no significant changes** across different filter lifecycle stages or water types2. Mature, established gut ecosystems possess colonization resistance that limits perturbation by transient water-borne heterotrophs.  
> * **Low-Disruption Chlorination in High-Contamination Environments**: Point-of-collection drinking water chlorination in Bangladeshi households produced **only minor, non-disruptive shifts** in overall pediatric gut microbiota diversity and resistome structures8. High background environmental microbial exposure can overwhelm subtle disinfectant-induced compositional shifts.  
> * **Dissociation of Commensal Shifts from Pathogen Burden**: Household free chlorine residuals in Haitian drinking water supplies significantly enriched beneficial *Bifidobacterium* species but showed **no statistically significant correlation** with enteric pathogen burden or clinical diarrheal incidence9.  
> * **Chemical Water Exposure vs. Intestinal Permeability Biomarkers**: High perfluoroalkyl substance (PFAS) exposure through municipal drinking water showed **no statistically significant association** with fecal markers of gut permeability (zonulin) or intestinal inflammation (calprotectin) in a community cohort15.

## **Research Gaps and Proposed Empirical Study Designs**

### **Identified Gaps in Current Literature**

> 1. **POU Biofilm to Systemic Inflammaging Pipeline**: No longitudinal clinical study has isolated domestic POU carbon filter effluent (measuring heterotrophic plate counts, *Sphingomonas*, *Pseudomonas*, and endotoxin levels) and systematically tracked host biological ageing clocks (DNAm GrimAge, PhenoAge) or chronic systemic inflammatory cytokines (hs-CRP, IL-6, TNF-α) in healthy adult populations.  
> 2. **Gut Barrier Kinetics under Pure Heterotrophic/Biofilm Exposure**: Existing gut permeability literature focuses on acute enteric pathogens or environmental enteropathy6. The physiological impact of consuming non-pathogenic, high-density biofilm fragments (![][image3] to ![][image4] CFU/mL of *Comamonadaceae*, *Brevundimonas*, or aquatic amoebae) on intestinal tight-junction expression (ZO-1, occludin) in healthy adult mucosa remains unmeasured.

### **Proposed Empirical Study Designs**

#### **Study Design 1: Double-Blind Human RCT on POU Biofilm Water, Gut Permeability, and Inflammaging**

> * **Objective**: Test whether long-term consumption of unchlorinated, high-heterotroph POU filter dispenser water alters gut mucosal permeability, circulating endotoxins, and systemic inflammaging markers in healthy adults.  
> * **Cohort**: 160 healthy adults (aged 45–65 years) randomly assigned 1:1 into two parallel arms for 12 months.  
> * **Interventions**:  
  * *Arm A (Biofilm Exposure)*: Domestic water dispenser fitted with a standard carbon block filter operated past its recommended service volume to deliver ![][image3]–![][image2] CFU/mL heterotrophic bacteria (chlorine-free).  
  * *Arm B (Control)*: Identical domestic water dispenser fitted with an inline 0.2 µM absolute microfiltration capsule and ultraviolet (UV) disinfection unit delivering sterile, chlorine-free water (\<10 CFU/mL).  
> * **Primary Outcome Measures**:  
  * *Intestinal Permeability*: Dual-sugar urinary excretion assay (Lactulose/Mannitol ratio) and serum Zonulin measured at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months.  
  * *Inflammaging & Biological Ageing*: High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), IL-6, serum LPS-binding protein (LBP), and DNAm GrimAge epigenetic clock acceleration derived from blood DNA methylation profiling at baseline and 12 months.  
  * *Gut Microbiome*: Shotgun metagenomic sequencing of stool samples to track water-borne strain colonization and functional metabolic pathways.

#### **Study Design 2: Controlled Murine Model of Chronic Biofilm Fragment Ingestion**

> * **Objective**: Determine the cellular mechanisms by which POU filter biofilm fragments influence mucosal tight-junction integrity and senescent cell accumulation in aging intestinal tissue.  
> * **Model**: Aged C57BL/6 mice (18 months old at baseline), n=20 per group, treated for 24 weeks.  
> * **Interventions**:  
  * *Group 1*: Standard autoclaved drinking water control.  
  * *Group 2*: Autoclaved water spiked with isolated POU carbon filter biofilm lysate / live heterotrophic isolate mixture (*Comamonadaceae*, *Brevundimonas*, *Sphingomonas*) at ![][image2] CFU/mL equivalent.  
  * *Group 3*: Autoclaved water spiked with low-dose purified aquatic bacterial endotoxin (100 EU/mL).  
> * **Primary Outcome Measures**:  
  * *In Vivo Permeability*: Oral FITC-dextran (4 kDa) mucosal flux assay measured in plasma.  
  * *Tissue Histology & Senescence*: Immunofluorescence quantification of colonic/ileal ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-1 tight junctions; RT-qPCR and RNA-seq quantification of cellular senescence markers (![][image10], ![][image11], SASP cytokines IL-1β, TNF-α) in isolated intestinal epithelial cells.  
  * *Microbiome*: 16S rRNA gene profiling of cecal and mucosal-associated bacterial communities.
