# Pilot: does the oral microbiome follow the water? (one person, blinded)

Written for: one person doing the blinded water swap, with a helper, who also wants an objective measure from the mouth. This is a plan for a test, not a finding. It is unvalidated and not medical advice. If you have persistent bad breath, gum bleeding or any symptoms that worry you, see a dentist or doctor first.

## The question

When the same person drinks first-draw fridge-dispenser water for a fortnight and clean tap water for a fortnight, **in a blinded order**, do the targeted oral bacteria change in the same direction each time? We are not asking whether the fridge water "causes" anything. We are asking whether there is a repeatable, measurable difference.

## Why this design

- **Same person, so lasting differences cancel out.** Brushing habits, smoking, age and diet affect the mouth much more than water does. Comparing you against other people can't separate those. Comparing you against yourself can.
- **Repeated, because single tests wobble.** Oral microbiome results can shift between tests even when nothing changed. One before-and-after pair tells us very little. We want several.
- **Blinded, so expectation can't drive it.** The helper keeps the key sealed until the end, as in the water-swap handout.

## What to measure

Use a **quantitative PCR panel of oral bacteria**, ordered through a practitioner, with a saliva and swab collection kit. A panel like this measures a fixed list of organisms. It does **not** read the whole community, so it can't discover new species. Check the exact list when you order, and choose the target list below **before** you see any results.

**Primary targets (decide these in advance, no more than five):** the organisms that make sulphur gas on the tongue and gums, which are the ones our bad-breath idea is about:
1. *Fusobacterium nucleatum*
2. *Porphyromonas gingivalis*
3. *Treponema denticola*
4. *Prevotella intermedia*
5. *Tannerella forsythia*

**Secondary targets, reported but not used to decide:** *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Acinetobacter* species, *Candida* species, and any other organism on the panel that also lives in dispenser water or biofilm.

Why the limit: the more organisms we compare, the more likely one will look different by chance. Five pre-chosen primaries keep that in check.

Also record, every sampling day: the handheld breath sulphide meter reading (see the water-swap handout) and the 0 to 3 symptom score.

## What the lab's sample report shows (read before ordering)

We read the lab's sample report for this panel (a made-up patient). What matters for the pilot:

- **Not accredited.** The report says the test "is currently not under the laboratory's scope of accreditation", although the lab holds accreditation for other tests. So treat it as an unaccredited research measurement, and say so whenever the results are shared.
- **Saliva, not a tongue swab.** The specimen is saliva, measured by quantitative PCR, plus a pH electrode reading. Saliva carries bacteria from the tongue and gums, but it isn't a measure of the tongue coat itself.
- **Reported against thresholds.** Each organism gets a number in its own units (powers of ten of "CFU per mL", converted from the PCR signal) and a high or low flag against a reference value. The report does not give the detection limit, the repeatability, or any uncertainty.
- **Many results come back "<DL", below the detection limit.** If most of our five primary targets are below the limit at baseline, they can't show a fall, and a rise would only show once it crosses the limit. **Look at the baseline first.** If all five are <DL, this panel is the wrong measure for you, and we should say so before spending on six more kits.
- **The interpretive comments are not evidence.** They link single organisms to heart disease, pregnancy outcomes and "acidemia" (a blood condition that saliva pH doesn't measure), and suggest probiotic supplements. We use only the numbers, never the commentary.
- **Saliva pH** is a cheap extra number, but it changes with eating, drinking and time of day, so it is only comparable if every sample is taken the same way.

**Ask the lab, in writing, before you order:** the detection limit and units for each of our five primary targets; the repeatability at low levels; whether the result is a quantity or only above or below a cut-off; and how they store and transport the sample.

**First sample: take two.** Collect two samples on the same morning, 10 minutes apart, and send both. The difference between them is the test's own noise. Any change in the pilot should be clearly larger than that gap before we call it real.

## The schedule

Three blocks of each water, alternating in a coin-flip order chosen by the helper (A is fridge water in some blocks, B in others). Each block runs **two weeks**, with a **one-week washout** on clean tap water between blocks. That's about 15 weeks.

| Sample | When | Which water |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline | Start of week 1 | Usual water, before anything changes |
| After each block | Last morning of each two-week block (6 samples) | The block's coded water |
| After each washout | Last morning of each washout week | Clean tap water |

This is a lot of kits. If cost is the limit, drop the washout samples and keep the baseline plus the six end-of-block samples, **seven in total**, and say so in the write-up.

## How to take every sample

- Same time of day, **first thing in the morning, before eating, drinking, brushing, mouthwash or smoking.**
- Same method each time, exactly as the kit says. Note the time.
- Don't change anything else for the whole study: toothpaste, mouthwash, floss, diet and supplements. **Write down any antibiotic, illness, dental visit or new medicine**, because they can swamp the result.
- Keep the water the same each block: all your plain drinking water comes from the coded bottles.
- Label by code only (for example "OM-03"). The helper holds the key.

## Decide the rule before you look

These are **our suggestions, not a published standard**. Write down your own, sign and date it, and give it to the helper first.

- **A match** is when, for **at least four of the five primary targets**, the level is higher after the fridge-water block than after the neighbouring clean-water block in **all six pairs**. For one target, six out of six in one direction happens by chance about 1 time in 64 (1 in 32 if you count either direction). The five targets tend to rise and fall together, so don't multiply those odds. Treat a "match" as a reason for a bigger study, not as proof.
- **No match** is a result worth publishing too.
- **Check the breath meter** and the symptom score against the oral results. If they agree, it is stronger. If they disagree, say so.

## What this can and can't show

- **It can show** whether specific mouth bacteria rise and fall with the water in one person.
- **It can't show** that the fridge caused the change, that the bacteria came from the water, or that anything is harmful. For that we would need the water tested too (plates, ATP, or sequencing) and the bacteria matched to it.
- **It is one person.** A pattern across several people would be needed before anyone should rely on it.
- **A panel of 20 organisms is a narrow window.** Something could change outside it.
- **Marketing language isn't evidence.** Lab pages for these panels often list diseases the organisms are linked to and claim reversibility through lifestyle. We don't use those claims. We use the panel only as a measurement.

## Optional: a cheap plate alongside (a rough extra, not a substitute)

A swab onto an agar plate costs very little and could be taken at every sampling. It is a much cruder measure than the PCR panel, and it follows the sealed-plate rules in [agar_plate_protocol.md](agar_plate_protocol.md).

- **What it shows:** roughly how much grows from your mouth on these plates, and whether yeast shows up on the fungal plate. It can't tell you which bacteria they are, and **you shouldn't try to identify anything at home.** White or cream colonies on a fungal plate don't mean Candida.
- **What it misses:** most mouth bacteria like body warmth and little oxygen, and don't grow on R2A at room temperature. So the plate shows only the part of the mouth community that happens to grow under those conditions. A change in the PCR panel can be real even when the plate shows nothing.
- **Take it second.** Collect the PCR sample first, exactly as the kit says, then the plate swab from the same place.
- **Standardise it, or it means nothing.** Use a new sterile swab each time, the same area of the back of the tongue, the same number of strokes, the same pressure. Then streak one R2A and one fungal plate in the same pattern.
- **Controls, every session:** a sterility control plate of each kind, and a "blank" swab that touches nothing but the air, streaked onto a plate. Any growth on these means that session can't be trusted.
- **Sealed, at room temperature only.** Don't incubate at body temperature, because that favours organisms that live in people. Never open the plates. Photograph on days 3, 5 and 7 with the same light and a ruler in shot.
- **Treat every plate as containing human bacteria.** Wear gloves, keep them closed and taped, and dispose of them as in section 7 of the agar protocol. If anyone in the household has a weak immune system, keep the plates out of shared spaces.
- **Don't over-read it.** Count only the 7-day photo, use the same counting rule each time, and have a second person count from the photos without seeing the first count. Differences of less than about threefold are within the noise.

## Pair it with the water

If you can, test the water you drank in each block:
- **Plates and chlorine** on the first-draw bottle, as in the replication protocol.
- **Keep a small sealed sample** of each block's water cold, in case a later test (for example sequencing) becomes possible.

## Privacy and sharing

- Keep the results anonymous when you share: a code, no name, no address.
- Share the raw numbers, good or bad, and the signed rule you set in advance.
- If more people join later, get consent and consider an ethics review before publishing.
